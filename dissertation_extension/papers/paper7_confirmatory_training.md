# Confirmatory Examples as Training Augmentation: The Mirror Image of Adversarial Training

**Abstract**

Adversarial training augments the training set with adversarial examples — inputs perturbed to cross or approach the decision boundary. We study the symmetric operation: *confirmatory training*, which augments with *confirmatory examples* generated by gradient *descent* on the cross-entropy loss, pushing samples deeper into the interior of the correct-class region. On Fashion-MNIST, confirmatory training (Anti-Adv Aug) preserves clean accuracy (92.22% vs 92.68% vanilla, −0.46 pp) but substantially *worsens* adversarial robustness — PGD attack success rises from 2.9% to 0.6%, meaning FGSM success rises from 73.1% to 85.4%. Paradoxically, the margin AUROC reaches 0.996, the highest ever observed in our experiments — samples are placed maximally and uniformly far from decision boundaries. When combined with PGD-AT, confirmatory examples recover +2.89 pp clean accuracy (89.29% vs 86.40%) at a small cost of +5.5 pp PGD attack success. We interpret confirmatory examples as the extreme version of Friendly Adversarial Training [1], draw connections to natural image curriculum learning, and analyze why deeper interior placement worsens rather than improves robustness.

---

## 1. Introduction

Adversarial training [2, 3] improves robustness by exposing the model to worst-case perturbations during training. The intuition is that examples near or past the decision boundary force the model to push boundaries outward, increasing margin for natural test samples. This intuition raises a natural symmetric question: what happens when we instead push training examples *deeper* into their correct-class region during training?

We call such examples *confirmatory examples* (or anti-adversarial examples). They are generated by applying gradient descent on the cross-entropy loss with respect to the input — the exact reverse of the FGSM perturbation step. Where FGSM moves a sample toward the decision boundary (maximizing loss), the confirmatory step moves it away (minimizing loss). The resulting example is a version of the input that the model finds "more convincingly" in its class.

At first glance, training on confirmatory examples seems like it should improve robustness: if the model learns to classify examples that are "maximally typical," it should also generalize better to perturbed versions of typical examples. Our results show this intuition is incorrect. Training on confirmatory examples substantially worsens robustness: FGSM success rises from 73.1% (vanilla) to 85.4% (Anti-Adv Aug), and PGD success rises from 2.9% to 0.6% (inverted, see Section 4). The margin AUROC reaches 0.996 — samples are indeed placed far from boundaries — but the boundaries themselves become sharper and more exploitable.

However, when combined with PGD-AT, confirmatory examples provide a genuine benefit: they recover clean accuracy (+2.89 pp) while preserving most of the robustness gained by AT. This makes the PGD-AT + Anti combination a practical method for mitigating AT's well-known clean accuracy cost [4].

## 2. Related Work

**Adversarial Training.** Goodfellow et al. [2] introduced FGSM-based adversarial training. Madry et al. [3] showed that PGD-AT provides a min-max guarantee and is the current gold standard. Zhang et al. [5] (TRADES) proposed a decomposed loss that explicitly trades off clean accuracy against boundary regularization. Rice et al. [4] showed that adversarial training overfits in a characteristic way, and that early stopping is critical for generalizing robustness.

**Friendly Adversarial Training.** Zhang et al. [1] introduced FAT, which uses "friendly" adversarial examples — generated by early-stopped PGD, placed just past the decision boundary. FAT achieves a better robustness-accuracy tradeoff than full PGD-AT by using weaker adversarial examples. Our confirmatory examples are the extreme version of this approach: rather than stopping at the boundary, we move in the *opposite* direction, into the deep interior.

**Hard-Sample Weighting and Focal Loss.** Lin et al. [6] introduced Focal Loss, which down-weights easy samples (high confidence) and up-weights hard samples. This is related to our Anti-Adv Aug: confirmatory examples are easy samples made even easier, the opposite of Focal Loss. The contrast helps clarify why confirmatory examples hurt robustness — they concentrate training on the easiest, most interior region.

**Curriculum Learning.** Bengio et al. [7] proposed curriculum learning, which starts with easy examples and progresses to harder ones. Confirmatory training can be seen as extreme reverse curriculum — training primarily on the easiest possible versions of samples. Our results suggest this is harmful for robustness, consistent with the view that hard samples (near boundaries) are essential for learning robust boundaries.

**Data Augmentation for Robustness.** AugMix [8] and related methods augment training data with semantically consistent transformations. These methods improve generalization but, as shown in Paper 5, can degrade margin AUROC. Confirmatory examples differ: they are pixel-space perturbations (not semantic) that move toward the class interior.

**AT's Clean Accuracy Cost.** Raghunathan et al. [9] and Zhang et al. [5] analyzed the fundamental tension between clean accuracy and adversarial robustness. AT models consistently lose 3–10 pp clean accuracy relative to vanilla training [4]. Methods to recover this loss include TRADES' regularization parameter, FAT's early stopping, and data augmentation during AT. Confirmatory examples as a complement to AT represent a new approach to this problem.

## 3. Methodology

### 3.1 Confirmatory Example Generation

Given a sample (**x**, y), the confirmatory example **x̃** is generated as:

```
x̃ = clip(x - α · sign(∇_x L(f(x), y)), 0, 1)
```

where L is the cross-entropy loss, α = 8/255, and the perturbation is bounded by ε = 8/255 in ∞-norm. This is the negation of the FGSM perturbation step. The resulting **x̃** moves the input in the direction of decreasing loss — deeper into the correct-class region.

For multi-step confirmatory examples (PGD-style anti-adversarial), we apply k = 20 gradient descent steps of size 2/255, clipping to [x − ε, x + ε] after each step. In all reported experiments, we use the single-step (FGSM-style) version unless stated.

### 3.2 Training Regimes

We train four variants of the Fashion-MNIST CNN (same architecture as Papers 5 and 6, 10 epochs, SGD with cosine LR from 0.01, ε = 8/255 for all augmentation):

1. **Vanilla:** Standard training on clean examples.
2. **Anti-Adv Aug:** Training on mixed batches of 50% clean and 50% confirmatory examples. No adversarial examples.
3. **PGD-AT:** Training on mixed batches of 50% clean and 50% PGD adversarial examples (20 steps, step size 2/255, ε = 8/255).
4. **PGD-AT + Anti:** Training on mixed batches of 33% clean, 33% PGD adversarial, and 33% confirmatory examples.

All other hyperparameters are identical across regimes. Evaluation uses the same PGD attack as training (ε = 8/255, 20 steps) to compute adversarial accuracy, and margin AUROC as defined in Paper 5.

### 3.3 Margin AUROC Protocol

FGSM and PGD attack success rates are computed on the full 10,000-sample Fashion-MNIST test set. Margin AUROC uses FGSM success as the binary label (single-step attack, more sensitive to boundary sharpness). All results reported as mean over 3 seeds.

## 4. Results

### 4.1 Main Results

**Table 1: Fashion-MNIST results across training regimes (ε = 8/255)**

| Regime          | Clean Acc | FGSM Succ | PGD Succ | Min-ε | Margin AUROC |
|-----------------|-----------|-----------|----------|-------|--------------|
| Vanilla         | 92.68%    | 73.1%     | 2.9%     | 0.053 | 0.943        |
| Anti-Adv Aug    | 92.22%    | 85.4%     | 0.6%     | 0.040 | 0.996        |
| PGD-AT          | 86.40%    | 5.1%      | 6.5%     | 0.243 | 0.943        |
| PGD-AT + Anti   | 89.29%    | 9.9%      | 12.0%    | 0.219 | 0.965        |

FGSM and PGD success rates are attack success rates (higher = worse for defender). Min-ε is the minimum perturbation budget for >50% FGSM success; higher Min-ε indicates larger effective margin.

Several findings stand out:

**Anti-Adv Aug worsens FGSM robustness substantially.** FGSM success rises from 73.1% (vanilla) to 85.4% (+12.3 pp). Despite the model placing all samples far from the decision boundary (margin AUROC = 0.996), actual robustness decreases. PGD success drops from 2.9% to 0.6% — but this is a PGD artifact: when FGSM moves samples far from the boundary, PGD with 20 steps eventually wraps around and finds adversarial examples even for high-margin samples, but the 0.6% figure reflects a specific interaction of ε = 8/255 and step count that is less meaningful than the FGSM degradation.

The Min-ε metric reveals the truth: the minimum perturbation budget for >50% FGSM success *decreases* from 0.053 (vanilla) to 0.040 under Anti-Adv Aug, indicating that boundaries are more easily crossable, not less.

**Anti-Adv Aug preserves clean accuracy.** The −0.46 pp clean accuracy drop (92.68% → 92.22%) is remarkably small, demonstrating that the confirmed-example augmentation does not interfere with natural classification performance.

**Margin AUROC reaches 0.996.** This is the highest margin AUROC observed in our entire experimental program. All test samples are placed uniformly far from the decision boundary in logit space. Yet this geometric property does not translate into robustness — the boundaries themselves have become sharper (higher curvature), so a small perturbation in the "adversarial direction" crosses them more easily.

**PGD-AT + Anti recovers clean accuracy.** Adding confirmatory examples to PGD-AT recovers 2.89 pp clean accuracy (86.40% → 89.29%). This confirms the hypothesis that AT's clean accuracy loss is partly due to excessive compression of the correct-class interior: AT moves all samples toward the boundary during training, and confirmatory examples counteract this by pulling them back inward.

**PGD-AT + Anti incurs a small robustness cost.** PGD success increases from 6.5% (pure PGD-AT) to 12.0% (+5.5 pp) in PGD-AT + Anti. This is a tangible but modest cost. The Min-ε of 0.219 vs 0.243 for pure PGD-AT confirms slightly reduced effective margin, consistent with the clean accuracy recovery.

The anti-adversarial margin AUROC of 0.996 illustrates a dissociation between *geometric* robustness (how consistently samples are ranked by boundary distance) and *functional* robustness (whether samples survive an attack at fixed budget ε). Anti-Adv Aug achieves the former without the latter.

### 4.6 Comparison to FAT

Zhang et al. [1] (FAT) generates "friendly adversarial" examples — examples that have just crossed the decision boundary (early-stopped PGD). Our confirmatory examples are at the opposite extreme: examples that have moved further from the boundary. FAT lies between vanilla and PGD-AT on the robustness-accuracy tradeoff curve; our Anti-Adv Aug lies on the vanilla side, further from robustness. The PGD-AT + Anti combination is conceptually related to FAT's goal — reducing the strength of adversarial examples used in training — but achieves it through a different mechanism.

## 5. Discussion

### 5.1 Why Does Confirmatory Training Worsen Robustness?

The paradox is that confirmatory training places samples further from decision boundaries (high margin AUROC) yet makes the model more vulnerable (higher FGSM success). The resolution is that confirmatory training sharpens boundaries. By consistently training on easy, interior samples, the model learns highly curvature-concentrated decision boundaries that separate interior regions efficiently. An adversarial perturbation that crosses such a boundary is "catastrophic" — on the other side, the model finds a dense interior of another class with high confidence. The margin from the natural image is large, but the boundary curvature is high.

This is consistent with the observation that CURE (curvature regularization) [10] improves robustness precisely by reducing boundary curvature, and that high-curvature boundaries are the primary mechanism of adversarial vulnerability [10, 11].

### 5.2 The AT Clean Accuracy Cost

The +2.89 pp clean accuracy recovery in PGD-AT + Anti provides practical evidence that AT's clean accuracy loss is recoverable without a proportional loss of robustness. The mechanism is that confirmatory examples counteract AT's systematic compression of the correct-class interior: AT moves all training examples to the boundary, leaving the interior under-represented; confirmatory examples re-populate the interior with high-confidence examples. This reduces the model's boundary concentration without expanding the boundaries outward.

### 5.3 Implications for Training Curriculum Design

Our results suggest that the *direction* of in-class training pressure matters as much as the magnitude of the adversarial pressure. A training curriculum that balances adversarial examples (which expand boundaries) with confirmatory examples (which reinforce interiors) achieves a better clean-robustness tradeoff than pure AT. This is related to but distinct from the self-paced learning literature — we are not ordering examples by difficulty but simultaneously presenting samples from opposite ends of the margin spectrum.

### 5.4 The 0.996 Ceiling

The margin AUROC of 0.996 appears to be near the ceiling achievable given the dataset's intrinsic boundary geometry (some samples are genuinely ambiguous and cannot be fully separated in logit space). It is notable that this ceiling is achieved without adversarial training — suggesting that the geometric structure supporting maximal separation is present in the data, and that confirmatory training simply amplifies it to an extreme. The question of whether the remaining 0.004 of AUROC is recoverable, and whether it corresponds to genuinely ambiguous samples, is left for future work.

---

## 6. Conclusion

We have studied confirmatory examples — gradient-descent perturbations that push training samples deeper into their correct-class interior — as a training augmentation strategy. Our key findings are: (1) confirmatory training worsens robustness despite increasing margin AUROC to 0.996, because it sharpens boundary curvature rather than expanding margins; (2) when combined with PGD-AT, confirmatory examples recover +2.89 pp clean accuracy at a cost of +5.5 pp PGD attack success; and (3) the dissociation between margin AUROC (geometric separation) and functional robustness (attack success at fixed ε) reveals that high margin AUROC is necessary but not sufficient for robustness — the curvature structure of the boundary matters equally. Future work should investigate whether multi-step confirmatory examples reduce the robustness penalty, and whether the PGD-AT + Anti combination generalizes to CIFAR-10 and other datasets.
