  x2 = 0.4*z + 0.917*noise  [income proxy, r≈0.4 with z]
Both features have unit variance by construction.
======================================================================

KEY INSIGHT:
  Neither x1 nor x2 is the 'true' feature. A human expert knows x1
  captures credit behaviour (noisy) and x2 captures income (noisier).
  The model must learn a weighted combination. Adversarial attacks
  exploit this combination — a small push along the decision boundary
  direction flips the class even though neither feature changed enough
  to be humanly meaningful.

Bayes-optimal direction: [0.7291, 0.6844]  (= [0.7, 0.4] / norm)
TDI = cosine(input gradient, Bayes direction)
  High |TDI| → attack aligned with latent structure (principled)
  Low  |TDI| → attack exploits noise orthogonal to z (spurious)

INTERPRETATION
  The model cannot observe z directly; it must use a weighted combination
  of x1 and x2. Adversarial examples push along the model's decision
  boundary, which is oblique (diagonal) because both features contribute.

  For cross-entropy models, the decision boundary lies in data-dense
  regions. Small, imperceptible perturbations suffice to cross it
  — adversarial examples appear identical to humans ('invisible to human').

  Gradient-penalty regularization pushes the boundary into sparser regions.
  Successful attacks require larger, human-visible changes.
  This is the core prediction of the Geometric Blind Spot theorem.
